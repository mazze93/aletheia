## 1. The predecessor, and why it needed a successor

`vault-rag` compiles Markdown into a knowledge wiki. It worked. Two things were
wrong with it, one operational and one structural.

**Operational.** The dashboard would not launch from the `daedalus` account, for
two independent reasons: `~`-relative source roots expand against whoever runs
the process, so a config written by `mazze` retargeted every path at the wrong
home; and the tool's own directory was not group-writable. Mazze launched it via
`sudo -u mazze` and it came up on :4321 with real data.

**Structural, and the reason for everything that followed.** Mazze:

> when claims made in the write ups are incorrect, there is no method for me to
> contest them or abjudicate

The compiled pages carried the footer *"do not hand-edit — the next compile
overwrites this file."* A human correction had a lifespan of one recompile. The
documented recourse — edit the sources — addresses only the case where a source
is wrong, and the common case is that the sources are right and the *synthesis*
is wrong.

Its one dispute signal was noise. Three pages were marked `contested`; all three
were the parser storing the model's literal `CONTRADICTION: None` reply as a
contradiction. **Zero real disputes, presented as three.**

## 2. palimpsest

Built as the successor: a third tier, `rulings/`, authored and append-only,
living outside the wiki and outside the SQLite index so a verdict survives
`rm -rf wiki` and `rm index.sqlite3`.

Design choices worth remembering:

- **Rulings are applied by the renderer, not requested of the model.** An 8B
  model asked not to restate a claim will restate it. Enforcement that depends
  on model compliance is not enforcement, and it fails silently.
- **A struck claim is marked, not deleted.** Deleting would let the next compile
  reintroduce it onto a page showing no sign of the dispute.
- **Claims are keyed on normalized content**, so a resynthesis that rewords a
  claim does not silently revoke the verdict attached to it.

Verified by the test that justifies the project: contest a claim → `rm -rf` the
entire wiki → recompile → the strike is still there.

Stdlib-only, plus Ollama. The compiler ran on-device throughout, deliberately —
cloud budget was at 97% when the build started.

**Local model review, and what it was worth.** llama3.1:8b was pointed at
`rulings.py` and returned 10 findings. All 10 were false positives; three
asserted that checks did not exist when they existed and were tested, and one
would have broken the documented append-only contract. Conclusion carried
forward: an 8B model is poor at *contract* reasoning but earned its place as the
compiler runtime. Future local passes should be mechanical and checkable.

## 5. The finding that changed the plan

She was about to be wired as designed: `PreToolUse` on `Bash|Edit|Write`, exit 2
blocks. I tested 8 payloads, saw zero blocks, and called it safe.

That proof was hollow. All 8 were bare commands, which sample the one region
where nothing happens. Probing with realistic content:

| payload | score | as designed |
|---|---|---|
| `npm audit fix` | 0.25 | proceed |
| commit message with a version table | 0.49 | warn |
| **the Dependabot PR body from earlier that night** | **0.82** | **blocked** |
| editing aletheia's own README | **0.82** | **blocked** |

Wired as designed, she would have blocked the shipping of the security fix from
section 3. The mechanism, stated plainly: **remediation work is linguistically
identical to injection.** Both name versions, both carry urgency, both instruct.

So the wiring changed, and so did the architecture:

1. `assess()` classifies and explains. It does not decide.
2. `policy.py` is the only module that may block, from provenance and carried
   taint — score is one input.
3. `unclassified` is not `untrusted`. Unknown origin means audit and ask.
4. **Shadow mode by default.** Every outcome exits 0; the decision that *would*
   have been enforced is recorded with a content hash, not the payload.

## 6. Parity, and what it found

The promise in `assess.py` was prose: *"if you change a weight, a threshold, or
a pattern here, change it there too."* No build can keep that.

Rather than invent a scheme, borrowed `cognitive/stratum`'s: a reference
implementation, a committed golden projection, and CI that regenerates and
diffs. Mazze's instinct to look at stratum was right and saved the design work.

**It found a real evasion on its first run.** JavaScript's `\w` is ASCII-only
and Python's is not, so `café@1.2.3` was a governing parameter to one
implementation and invisible to the other.

Widening the corpus to probe every family **inverted the finding**: in 11 cases
it was the *Python* side that was blind — `criticalé` scored **0.0** — and
Python is what the hook actually runs.

## 7. Closing the class

Fixing eleven fixtures would have fixed eleven fixtures. Instead:

- `boundary.py` / `boundary.ts` state the rule explicitly — *match a keyword
  unless it is embedded in a larger ASCII identifier; adjacent Unicode letters
  do not suppress it* — and all 27 patterns in both languages are built on it.
- **651 mutation cases**: 21 detection atoms × letters from eight scripts ×
  combining marks, ZWSP/ZWNJ/ZWJ, RLO and BOM, at both boundaries, run against
  both runtimes in CI.
- The invariant that generalises it: **no Unicode-only mutation may lower the
  assessed risk.**
- An inventory gate: `\b` or `\B` reappearing in either assessor fails the build.

It discriminates rather than merely matching more — `criticalé` matches,
`hypercritical` and `critical_path` do not — which matters because false
positives are what forced shadow mode in the first place.

The mutation corpus immediately found a *second* dialect split the 69
hand-written vectors could not reach: ECMAScript counts **U+FEFF** as whitespace
and Python does not, so `\S+` captured a different *extent*.
